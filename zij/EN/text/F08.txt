The establishment of the text and the critical apparatus are on the whole the work of Bjørnbo. This scholar was of the opinion, as I learned from a letter of Prof. Heiberg to me, that the text of C was the best, and for this reason he generally took it as his basis. A renewed comparison of C and O[^XXIV.1], however, has shown me that in several places the reading of O is the more apt (a few times also that of N); in such cases I have therefore taken the liberty of adopting the better reading into the text. Where the reading of a manuscript is not given in the footnotes, it agrees with that of the text.
@p 0028L12..0028L24
The commentary that follows the text is entirely my own work. I first asked myself whether it might be necessary to follow the text with a German translation, since the subject is often set out very briefly and obscurely; but I told myself that the reader would be only half served by it, for whoever is not acquainted with ancient and medieval astronomy could not do much with a mere translation, and a commentary would have to be added to it as well. I have therefore done without the translation and contented myself with a commentary, which I have tried to make more useful by giving, for the chief astronomical problems treated in this work, examples solved by the rules of the text. — In the commentary I have translated the titles of the individual chapters of the text fairly literally; for convenient reference the chapters are numbered, which they are not in the manuscripts.
@p 0028L25..0028L32
I must confess that this commentary was a difficult and laborious work for me, and whoever reads through the individual chapters of the text will surely find this understandable. Here the excellent edition of al-Battānī, with the commentary on it, by C. A. Nallino and G. Schiaparelli[^XXIV.2] rendered me the most valuable services; without this edition a commentary on the present work would hardly have been possible for me. In spite of this help, however, several points have remained unclear, and I hope that scholars versed in these questions will perhaps lend their support to clearing them up.

In the commentary I have allowed myself to use abbreviations for the three proper names that occur most often, since this also makes the printing considerably easier: so I always write Kh. for al-Khwārizmī, Batt. for al-Battānī and Ptol. for Ptolemy.
@p 0029L01..0029L19
The work closes with two indexes, an *Arabic glossary* and a general *index of names and subjects*. The basis of the former was compiled by Dr. R. O. Besthorn in Copenhagen, to whom, together with J. L. Heiberg, we owe the edition of the oldest Arabic translation of Euclid. Various things were afterwards supplemented by me, in particular the correct forms of the Egyptian and Persian names of the months. I give the Arabic names only in transcription, first as they stand in the text and then in the correct form; in this I follow the rules of the *Encyclopaedia of Islam*, which is now appearing under the direction of Prof. Houtsma in Utrecht and others. For the reader I will only mention here that *dj* is to be pronounced like English *j*, *z* like a voiced *s*, *kh* like the Alemannic *ch*, *y* like English *y*, and *dh* and *th* like the voiced and the voiceless English *th*[^n]; the other signs need no explanation. The meaning of the Arabic words I have given in German[^n], with the agreement of Dr. Besthorn, who had originally added it in Latin. — As to the use of the glossary, I point out that in all words that begin with the article *al* or *el* the article is to be left out: look for *elmuht* under *muht*, for *eltheniae* under *theniae*, etc. — For words that recur very often, such as *dakaicae* (= minutae), *theniae* (= secundae) and a few others, I leave out the references to the pages, except those where their meaning is explained.
@n Suter gives the German equivalents: *dj* as *dsch*, *z* as soft *s*, *y* as *j*.
@n In this translation the meanings are given in English.
