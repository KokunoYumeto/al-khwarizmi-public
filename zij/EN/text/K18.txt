The moon, then, is 302° 50′ 56″ − 297° 4′ 32″ = 5° 46′ 24″ behind the sun, that is, the sun *praecessit* (has gone ahead), as the text puts it; the true conjunction therefore takes place only after the time found above, and, if the sun were regarded as standing still, by as many hours as the true hourly motion of the moon is contained in the difference of longitude 5° 46′ 24″. But since the longitude of the sun changes too, a correction is added to this difference, namely its 12th part = 28′ 52″, and the difference of longitude is now 6° 15′ 16″; now 6° 15′ 16″ ∶ 34′ 20″[^83.1] = 10$^{h}$ 55$^{m}$ 48$^{s}$; the *true conjunction*, then, took place on 29 Muḥ. 288 AH, 18$^{h}$ 44$^{m}$ 29$^{s}$ after the culmination of the sun, that is, on 23 January 901 at 6$^{h}$ 44$^{m}$ in the morning[^83.2].

The reason why $\tfrac{1}{12}$ of the difference of longitude must be added to the longitude of the sun, and $\tfrac{13}{12}$ of it to the longitude of the moon (in the case where the moon is ahead of the sun they must be subtracted), is surely that the mean hourly motions of the sun and the moon are nearly as 1 ∶ 13[^83.3]. Let *K* be the place of the true conjunction, *S* the place of the sun and *M* that of the moon, after the correction by their equations[^n]; then 13 parts fall to *MK* and one part to *SK*, and so 12 parts to *MS*. One therefore divides the difference of longitude *MS* by 12 and so has one part; adding this to the longitude of the sun, and thirteen times it to the longitude of the moon, one has the same true longitude of the two bodies at the time of the conjunction; and *MK* divided by the true hourly motion of the moon gives the number of hours that the two bodies still have to run from the positions *M* and *S* to the conjunction *K*. Since the ratio of the hourly motions of the two bodies is not constant, this procedure does not give quite exact results, and Batt. therefore remarks that his more lengthy procedure is the more correct one.

Two further precepts are now given, which are of no consequence for determining the conjunction, but are needed for the later computation of an eclipse that may occur. *First* it is remarked (p. 24) that the equation of the moon is also to be subtracted from (or added to) the argument of latitude; in our case this gives 167° 8′ − 4° 15′ 45″ = 162° 52′ 15″; and to this quantity the corrected difference of longitude (6° 15′ 16″) is to be added, which gives 169° 7′ 31″ as the corrected argument of latitude. *Secondly* it is added (pp. 24–25) that one must also correct the anomaly of the moon for the time correction found, 10$^{h}$ 55$^{m}$ 48$^{s}$; this is done by multiplying this quantity by 32′ 40″, that is, by the mean hourly motion of the anomaly of the moon; taking the decimal fraction 10.93 instead of 10$^{h}$ 55$^{m}$ 48$^{s}$, one obtains 5° 57′ 3″, and this, added to the anomaly (119° 10′)[^84.1], gives 125° 7′ = the corrected anomaly (in the text: *argumentum ratum*).
