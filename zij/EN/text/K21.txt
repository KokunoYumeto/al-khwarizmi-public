The rules of the *Sūrya-Siddhānta* on the determination of parallax are in its 5th chapter[^92.1]. In principle they do not differ essentially from Batt.'s: trigonometric relations are used there too, the so-called *meridian distance* and *ecliptic zenith distance* are computed, and from these the parallaxes in longitude and latitude are then determined.
@p 0124L14..0124L27
Kh.'s rules are much simpler; but since he only explains the use of the tables and gives no trigonometric derivations at all, it is difficult to decide by what route he found the numbers of his tables. His principle, briefly put, is the following: the *parallax* in *longitude* is a function of the deviation of the moon from the meridian to the east or to the west, that is, in our present-day way of speaking, a *function* of the *hour angle*; the *parallax* in *latitude* is a function of its *zenith distance*. The numbers in the first column (Table 77) therefore mean two things: if it is a matter of determining the *parallax* in *longitude*, they denote the distance of the moon from the meridian of the place of observation, measured on the equator (or on a parallel circle), and thus the *hour angle* of the moon; if it is a matter of determining the *parallax* in *latitude*, they mean the zenith distance of the moon, namely its so-called *ecliptic zenith distance*, that is, its zenith distance when it is in the *medium coelum*. — In this respect, then, Kh. seems to come nearer to the Indians than Batt. does.

The text of chapter 34 requires first the determination of the *ascendant* (*horoscopus*) for the given time of the conjunction, that is, of the longitude of the point of the ecliptic which — to take up again our *example* of a *conjunction* of the sun and the moon given above (pp. 82–84) — rises on 29 Muḥarram of the year 288 AH at 18$^{h}$ 44$^{m}$ 29$^{s}$. The text says nothing at all about how this ascendant is to be determined; nor, in our view, could it be found with the tables that this work contains, for the tables of the oblique ascensions are missing, of which we ought to have at least those for the latitude of Córdoba; it could, however, be determined by computation with the help of the ascensional difference. We have therefore determined the *ascendant* by Batt.'s precepts[^92.2] and found it = 297° 3′. Kh.'s rule for finding the *medium coelum* also seems to be faulty; at the least it is unclear from what the 90° are to be subtracted, whether from the *ascendant* itself, or from its *right ascension*, or from its *ascensio obliqua* (oblique ascension); we have therefore determined it likewise by Batt., and found it = 228° 40′. Now Kh.'s rules say that one must enter Tables 59–59$^{b}$, by which longitudes are converted into right ascensions and conversely, both with the *true longitude* of the moon (= 303° 20′) and with that of the *medium coelum* (= 228° 40′); doing this, one finds 305° 43′ for the right ascension of the moon and 226° 10′ for that of the medium coelum; the difference of the two, = 79° 33′, is the *hour angle* of the moon at the given time.[^93.1] For better understanding I give here the adjoining figure[^n], in which, so that it might agree better with our example, the intersection of the equator and the ecliptic that lies on the rear hemisphere had to be taken as the vernal point, and so the one that lies on the front hemisphere as the autumnal point *W*. The rule for finding the *parallax* in *latitude* is then a little simpler, since the questions of the *ascendant* and of the *medium coelum* are settled for us. It is now required to determine the *obliquatio* (declination) of the *medium coelum*, that is, the declination of the moon (= that of the sun) at that time; by Table 22 this is = − 19° 44′ 38″; we take the latitude of Córdoba as 37° 50′ in round numbers, and this gives for the ecliptic zenith distance *EZ* = 57° 34′ 38″; our text calls this the *interstitium medii coeli huius horoscopi et imminentis capitibus nostris* (the interval between the midheaven of this ascendant and the point that hangs over our heads). Now the *latitude* of the moon is to be determined: its corrected argument is, by p. 84, = 169° 7$\tfrac{1}{2}$′; to it belongs, by Table 26, the latitude 50′ 53″; it is northern, and is therefore to be subtracted from the ecliptic zenith distance found just now, which gives 56° 43′ 45″; with this argument one enters Table 77 and finds there the *parallax* of *latitude* = 40′ 43″, and it is to be taken as *southern*, because the *medium coelum* too lies south of the zenith of Córdoba.
