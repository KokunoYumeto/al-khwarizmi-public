2. Maslama b. Aḥmad al-Majrīṭī, Abū l-Qāsim, of Córdoba, lived in the time of al-Ḥakam II (961–976) and Hishām II (976–1013) and died in 1007/08. He was the most important mathematician and astronomer of Arab Spain in his time. His chief works are: on *commercial* or *business arithmetic* (*al-muʿāmalāt*), extant neither in Arabic nor in translation; on *the construction and use of the astrolabe*, still extant in Arabic in the Escorial; a translation of the *Planisphaerium* of Ptolemy with a commentary, translated into Latin by Hermannus Dalmata (Hermann of Dalmatia), printed at Basel in 1536 and at Venice in 1558; an *abridgement* of the *tables* of al-Battānī, no longer extant; the *reworking of the tables* of al-Khwārizmī, extant only in the Latin translation of Adelard of Bath, which we here make public.

We also give here, in a literal translation, the passage that the Spanish-Arab historian Ibn Ṣāʿid (d. 1070 in Toledo)[^IX.2] has handed down to us about Maslama's reworking of the tables of al-Khwārizmī[^IX.3]: “He occupied himself with the reworking of the tables of al-Khwārizmī; he converted their Persian chronology into the Arabic one and determined the mean places of the planets for the beginning of the *Hijra*; he added to the work further fine tables, in which, however, he followed him (that is, al-Khwārizmī) closely and did not draw attention to the errors in his work; I have pointed out this circumstance in my book ‘on the correction of the motion of the planets and the instruction on the errors of the astronomers’.”

This passage, then, proves to us that not all the tables in this recension come from al-Khwārizmī; but which are those that Maslama added is hard to decide. We have already drawn attention to this point above, and shall come back to it in the commentary. — By the remark that Maslama did not notice al-Khwārizmī's errors, or did not draw attention to them, he probably means in the first place the neglect of the precession of the equinoctial points.

The manuscripts of *Chartres*, Bibliothèque publique (C), and of *Oxford*, Bodleian Library (O), are both parchment manuscripts of the twelfth century, according to the authors of the catalogues; the former has the number 214 (173)[^X.3], the latter Cod. Auct. F. I. 9. They are on the whole well written and easy to read, C somewhat better than O. The written area of the leaf measures 26/18 cm in C and in O (from the photographs) 15/10 cm on average. Both manuscripts are fairly complete, but C contains several larger passages that are missing in O, and the other way round (see the text). Our treatise forms the end of C, from fol. 41$^{r}$ to 102$^{r}$; in O it occupies fol. 99$^{v}$ to 159$^{v}$, but fol. 159 has been moved to the end by mistake; it should stand between 99 and 100, so that the work really ends with fol. 158.

The manuscript of the Bibliothèque Mazarine in *Paris* (M)[^X.4], with the number 3642 (1258)[^X.5], is quite incomplete: instead of the 37 chapters of the text it contains only the first 21, which make up the smaller half of the text, and instead of c. 120 tables it has only 9. It too is a parchment manuscript, of the thirteenth century; the written area of the leaf measures 30/23 cm on average. Several hands have worked on it; Bjørnbo expressed this by the designations M$_{1}$, M$_{2}$, etc.[^XI.1] It is very probable that M is an incomplete and very careless copy of C, since even faulty readings mostly agree. Our treatise occupies fol. 82$^{r}$–89$^{v}$ of this manuscript. Besides it, the manuscript contains among other things: Thebit, *de iis quae indigent expositione antequam legatur Almagesti*, incomplete; Jordanus, *de pondere*; the treatise that begins *communis et consuetus* . . .[^XI.2]; *liber alchorismi arithmeticae qui editus est a magistro Johanne*.

We must deal somewhat more fully with the manuscript of the Biblioteca Nacional in *Madrid* (N), with the number 10016, which A. Bjørnbo unfortunately could no longer use, since it is of particular historical importance. According to the catalogue, whose particulars came to my knowledge through the efforts of Mr. Miguel Asín, professor of Arabic at the University of Madrid, to whom I here publicly express my most sincere thanks for his kind help, especially also in obtaining photographs of the manuscript, it is a parchment manuscript of the thirteenth century. The written area of the leaf measures, from the photographs, 16/10 cm in the text and 17/12 cm in the tables. The manuscript is very beautifully and evenly written and has few abbreviations; the initials are done in black, red and green, and the figures of the different columns of the tables and their titles also often (not throughout) alternate in black, red and green ink[^XI.3]. The manuscript was originally in the cathedral of *Toledo*. Prof. Asín remarked to me in a letter that it had probably come to Spain from Italy, since it had formed part of the collection of Cardinal Celada[^n], which had been brought together in Italy. We rather believe that it came into this collection only in Spain; the collection may well have been begun in Italy but perhaps continued in Spain.
