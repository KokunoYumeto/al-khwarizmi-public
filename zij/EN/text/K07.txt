But Chasles is mistaken, or makes a slip of the pen, when he says: *L’équation en question est précisément la moitié de l’équation du centre* (the equation in question is precisely half the equation of the centre); we have found that in Kh. the equation in question (that of the apogee) is *half the equation of the anomaly*. The equation of the anomaly (in our tables *Examinatio argumenti*) is halved; the one half is subtracted from the *apogee* as its equation (correction), and so gives the corrected apogee, which is entered in the second main column (see, for example, Table 27 for Saturn), headed *Sublimatio examinata*; the other half is probably used to correct the *equation* of the *centre*, but in what way we have not found out. We have made several tests, and in them have found this half now equal to the equation of the centre, now much greater, and a third time considerably smaller. I give two of these tests here, comparing the numbers in Kh. with the corresponding ones in Batt., which differ little from those of Ptol.

The reasons for this procedure, as already said, have not become clear to us; we have found out only so much, that one arrives at about the same true place of the planet whether one proceeds by Batt.'s method or by Kh.'s, provided that with the latter one takes the *precession* into account, which is just what he did not do. But in our work of commentary, and especially in this chapter on the true places of the planets, we have very much regretted that the “Reasons of the tables of Kh.” by al-Bīrūnī[^51.1], which still exist in the Hebrew translation of Ibn Ezra, have not yet been published.

Let us see further what the *Sūrya-Siddhānta* contains about determining the true place of the planets. Its rules[^51.2] are considerably more complicated than those of Kh., from which we may probably conclude that the Persian tables of the *Shāh* had already simplified the Indian method. I quote here only verses 43 and 44 (of book II) in the English translation of E. Burgess[^51.3]:
@p 0083L09..0083L11
43. *The process of correction for the apsis is the only one required for the sun and moon; for Mars and the other planets are prescribed that for the conjunction, that for the apsis, again that for the apsis and that for the conjunction — four in succession.*
@p 0083L12..0083L14
44. *To the mean place of the planet apply half the equation of the conjunction, likewise half the equation of the apsis; to the mean place of the planet apply the whole equation of the apsis and also that of the conjunction.*
@p 0083L15..0083L17
Without a commentary one could hardly do much with these rules; we see from them only so much, that a *halving* of the *equation* is involved here too. Burgess comments on these verses in the following way:
@p 0083L18..0083L26
*The phraseology of the text is not entirely explicit, and would bear, if taken alone, a different interpretation from that which the commentary puts upon it, and which the rules to be given later show to be its true meaning; this is as follows: first calculate from the mean place of the planet the equation of the conjunction, and apply the half of it to the mean place; from the position thus obtained calculate the equation of the apsis, and apply half of it to the longitude as already once equated; from this result find once more the equation of the apsis and apply it to the original mean place of the planet; and finally calculate from and apply to this last place the whole equation of the conjunction.*
@p 0083L27..0083L39 + 0084L01..0084L10
Burgess then gives examples of the computation of the true places of the five planets by these rules. We have spent a long time comparing these computations with those carried out by Kh.'s rules, but have not been able to find out the true connection. In the *Sūrya-Siddhānta* an equation is halved twice: first the *equation of the conjunction* (in our terms the *equation of the centre*) is halved, and half of this equation is added to the mean longitude of the planet; then the *equation of the apsis* (of the epicycle; in our terms the *equation of the anomaly*) is halved, and the half is again added to the new, corrected longitude of the planet; then the *equation of the anomaly* is determined anew from this twice corrected longitude, and it is added to the original mean longitude; finally the *equation of the centre* is determined from this result and added to the result just obtained, and so one has the *true longitude* of the planet. By these rules Burgess determined the true place of Saturn for 1 Jan. 1860 for the meridian of Washington, and found it c. 11° greater than the mean longitude; we have found it, by Kh.'s rules and with the same data as Burgess used, only c. 3° greater. This is so great a difference that one must almost suppose that Burgess did not understand or did not apply the rules of the *Sūrya-Siddhānta* correctly. In any case, however, the rules of the Persian tables of the *Shāh*, if Kh. really followed them, must, as has already been indicated, have departed considerably from those of the *Sūrya-Siddhānta*: of the *two* halvings of equations that Burgess found in the Indian tables, in our view only *one* has remained in Kh.
