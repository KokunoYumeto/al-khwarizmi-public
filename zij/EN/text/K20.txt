We have still to add here something about the determination of the size of the *earth's shadow* in eclipses of the moon. In the passage cited above (p. 79) al-Bīrūnī makes the further remark that the method of computing the size of the earth's shadow in eclipses of the moon is likewise the same in the *Khaṇḍakhādyaka* as in Kh. Of this method, however, we find nothing at all in our work; we have only, in Tables 61–66, two columns each (the 6th and the 7th), headed *Primum dimidium rotae draconis* and *Secundum dimidium rotae draconis* (first and second half of the wheel of the dragon), with which we shall deal directly. That those rules for determining the size of the earth's shadow are missing in this work may have its reason either in al-Bīrūnī's having had before him another edition of Kh.'s tables than Maslama al-Majrīṭī, or, what is more probable, in the present work's having been much abridged; whether by Maslama, or by the translator, or only by later copyists, remains undecided.

As regards the 6th and 7th columns of Tables 61–66, I have found the following. The numbers of the 6th column are 5 times the corresponding values of the hourly unequal motion (*buht*) of the sun, which are entered in column 2; the numbers of the 7th column are 1$\tfrac{3}{5}$ times the corresponding values of the hourly unequal motion of the moon, which are given in column 3; if one subtracts the values of column 6 from the corresponding ones of column 7, one obtains in each case the values of the radius of the earth's shadow that correspond to the anomalies of column 1. If, then, Kh. carried out these computations according to the *Khaṇḍakhādyaka*, as al-Bīrūnī reports, the rule of the Indian work must have run roughly as follows: subtract 5 times the hourly *buht* of the sun from 1$\tfrac{3}{5}$ times the hourly *buht* of the moon, and one has the apparent radius of the earth's shadow. *Example:* at an anomaly of 3$^{\text{sig.}}$ = 90° (Table 63, bottom line) the hourly *buht* of the sun (*motus solis in hora*, the motion of the sun in an hour) is = 2′ 28″, that of the moon = 32′ 56″; the former number multiplied by 5 gives 12′ 20″, and this is the corresponding number of the 6th column, that is, the *primum dimidium rotae draconis*; the second number multiplied by 1$\tfrac{3}{5}$ gives 52′ 42″, while the corresponding number of the 7th column, that is, the *secundum dimidium rotae draconis*, is = 52′ 47″[^90.1]; the apparent semidiameter of the earth's shadow at this anomaly is therefore = 52′ 47″ − 12′ 20″ = 40′ 27″.

Ptol.[^90.2] and after him Batt.[^90.3] state that the radius of the earth's shadow is c. 2$\tfrac{3}{5}$ times the apparent radius of the moon; in Kh. this factor is not quite constant: according to various trials that we have made, its values vary between 2.5 and 2.6, and so differ little from Ptol.'s value. This leads us to the conjecture that Brahmagupta, in his *Khaṇḍakhādyaka*, may perhaps have followed Ptol. in this question more than his Indian predecessors; at least the rules of the *Sūrya-Siddhānta*[^90.4] and of the *Pañcasiddhāntikā*[^90.5] for determining the size of the earth's shadow show no great kinship with that of Kh. In the first work the true diameters of the earth, the sun and the moon and the mean and true motions of the latter two heavenly bodies are drawn into the computation, and the result gives the true diameter of the earth's shadow in *yojanas*[^91.1], which can then, to be sure, easily be converted into minutes. In the second work the rule runs: “Multiply the true distance of the moon by 36, and divide this product by the true distance of the sun multiplied by 90 and divided by 276; subtract this quotient from 36, and one has the diameter of the earth's shadow (in *yojanas*).”

Kh.'s rules for determining the *parallaxes* of the moon in longitude and latitude are, in my view, somewhat corrupt, especially the former, or at least quite unclear. For orientation we state first that, since the determination of the parallax is used in this work only for computing the eclipses of the sun, the moon is assumed to be in the ecliptic when the parallax in longitude is determined, that is, its latitude is set equal to zero. It is further to be noted that, when the moon is in the meridian of the place of observation at the time of the conjunction, its parallax in longitude is equal to zero.
