On this trigonometrical chapter we have a few remarks to add to what Bjørnbo has already published about the table of sines and cotangents of this work.[^68.4]
@p 0101L01..0101L12
Kh. calls the sine of an angle *elgeib elmustewi* = *al-jayb al-mustawī*, and Adelard renders this by *elgeib planum* (he takes most Arabic words as neuters); *planus* is indeed one meaning of *mustawī*, but *rectus* = straight, or set straight upright, would be better here. Adelard, then, did not yet know (about the year 1126) the translation of *jayb* by the Latin *sinus*; on the other hand we see from the Madrid Ms. N that Robert of Chester, who probably carried out the better “ordering” of Adelard's translation between about 1143 and 1150, knew the word *sinus*. We do not know when Gerard of Cremona, who mostly did not date his translations, translated the astronomy of Jābir b. Aflaḥ, in which the word *sinus* occurs, and so we cannot decide whether he or Robert of Chester was the first to use this word; but the probability is greater for the latter.
@p 0101L13..0101L20
The *versed sine* Adelard calls *elgeib elmankūs* (or *maʿkūs*) = the reversed sine; but he translates *mankūs* wrongly by *diminutum*, confusing it with *manqūṣ*, which means “diminished”, “reduced”, and is pronounced almost the same. This may well be a proof that at that time one learned a language not so much from books as rather in spoken intercourse with people, and therefore in translating relied more on the spoken word than on the written or read one; other wrong spellings of Arabic words may well come from this too.
@p 0101L21..0101L23
Adelard's wrong translations, *mustawī* by *planus* and *mankūs* by *diminutus*, Robert of Chester did not correct; he too calls the sine *sinus planus* and the versed sine *sinus diminutus*.
