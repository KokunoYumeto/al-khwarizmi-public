Chapter 12. Determination of the longitude of the nodes of the moon
@c 0087L07
(Text p. 12, Tables 19 and 20.)
@p 0087L08..0087L13
As in Batt., only the ascending node (the dragon's head) is considered, because the longitude of the descending node (the dragon's tail) differs from it by 180°. The mean longitude is computed, like that of the sun and of the moon, from Tables 19 and 20 for any given moment; the result is then subtracted from 360°, because the motion of the nodes is retrograde, and the remainder is the true longitude of the node. I refrain from giving an example of this.
@h 0087L14
Chapter 13. On the stations and the forward and backward motion of the planets
@c 0087L15
(Text p. 12, Tables 27–56.)
@p 0087L16..0087L19
For these determinations Kh. does not set up tables of their own, as Batt. does (II, 138 and 139), but he has a special column for them in the tables of the equations of the planets, headed *Statio prima*; the column *Statio secunda* of Batt., which contains only the complements of the numbers of the *Statio prima* to 360°, Kh. leaves out.
@p 0087L20..0087L27
The rule is the following: for any given moment one determines, as in computing the true place of the planet, its *centrum aequat.* (*examinat.*), looks up the *statio prima* for this argument and subtracts it from 360°, and so has the *statio secunda*; then one also determines the *anomalia* (*elheza*) *examinata* of the planet at this moment. If it is equal to one of the two stations, the planet is stationary; if it lies between the two, that is, is greater than the first and less than the second, the planet is retrograde; in the other case (that is, when it is less than the first or greater than the second), it is moving forward.[^55.1]
@p 0087L28..0087L30
The rule in Batt. (I, 114) runs exactly the same, except that he leaves out (probably because he thought it obvious) the case where the anomal. exam. is equal to one of the two stations.
