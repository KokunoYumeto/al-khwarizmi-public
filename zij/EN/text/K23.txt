Of as great importance in astrology as the knowledge of the distribution of the twelve houses, or of greater importance still, was the determination of the *proiectiones radiorum* (projections of the rays), that is, of those points of the ecliptic that stand 60°, 90° or 120° from a given point in the heavens, for example from a planet, or, in other words, that stand with it in *sextile*, *square* or *trine aspect*. To these three *aspects* are then added the *conjunction* and the *opposition* (the *opposite aspect*). It is characteristic that Kh. devoted the greatest space of his tables precisely to this chapter: text and tables together take up 26 pages; this illustrates better than any other considerations or investigations the fact, known already, that the whole scientific work of antiquity[^99.1] and of the Middle Ages in the field of astronomy was not an end in itself, but had only to supply the means for the needs of astrology. It is well known, moreover, that even *Regiomontanus* wrote a work of his own on a related astrological subject[^99.2], in which he likewise (Problema 31) deals with the question of determining the *place* of the *casting of the rays* (*radiatio*).
@p 0131L11..0131L26
For determining these places too there were various routes, for whose details I must again refer to Batt.[^99.3]. The simplest were those that neglected the latitude of the planets, that is, assumed them to lie in the ecliptic; the most obvious thing then, naturally, was to go 60° (or 90° or 120°) forwards or backwards in the ecliptic from the planet, and one had the two points (the left and the right) of the sextile aspect (or of the square or the trine aspect): this primitive route was taken, for example, by al-Qabīṣī (Alcabitius) and Abraham ibn ʿEzra[^99.4]. Others, for example the Greek astrologer Dorotheus, are said, according to Ibn Hibintā, to have measured the place of the casting of the rays not on the ecliptic but on the equator; that is, they regarded as the radius of the sextile aspect the arc of the equator that was equal to the difference: right ascension of the planet minus right ascension of [longitude of the planet ± 60°], or conversely; in this way they came to the same point of the ecliptic as those named before. — Ptol. took a somewhat more complicated route in the *Quadripartitum*[^99.5], but likewise neglected the latitude of the planets. — The first to take it into account seems to have been Batt., who set up the formula

Which route, then, did Kh. take? From what Nallino[^100.1] quotes from Ibn Hibintā one would have to conclude that Kh. proceeded roughly as was reported above of Dorotheus, except that he converted the right ascension of the arc of the equator back into degrees of the ecliptic, and then took this arc of the ecliptic as the radius of the sextile aspect. But the tables before us here differ essentially, both in arrangement and in content, from Kh.'s tables as Ibn Hibintā has handed them down to us, a sample of which Nallino very kindly sent me: first, those go only from 10° to 10° of the signs, ours from 5° to 5°; secondly, they do not give in each case the whole oblique ascension[^n] counted from the vernal point, but only the oblique ascension of each sign by itself[^100.2]; the numbers for the sextile, square and trine aspects are also different. Our tables take as their basis the *ascensiones obliquae* (oblique ascensions) for the latitude of *Córdoba*, which Maslama gives as 38° 30′; Kh.'s tables for the *proiectiones radiorum* have therefore likewise been reworked for Córdoba. But now a difficult problem arises for us: the oblique ascensions agree in each case only with the numbers of the first three columns; after that they differ rather strongly.
