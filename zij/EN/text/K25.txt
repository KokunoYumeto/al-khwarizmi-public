Columns 3 and 5 contain the so-called *exaltations* (*dignitates*, *exaltationes*, Arab. *sharaf*, pl. *ashrāf*) and *dejections* (*deiectiones*, *casus*, Arab. *hubūṭ*), or rather the planets that are in exaltation or in dejection (fall) in the corresponding sign of the 1st column. Thus, for example, *Jupiter* is in *exaltation* in the *Crab* (*Cancer*), that is, in this sign he has the highest power, the greatest influence; on the other hand he is in *dejection* or in *fall* in the *Goat* (*Capricornus*), the sign that stands 180° from the Crab; in this sign he has the least influence, or none at all. *Saturn* is in *exaltation* in the *Balance*, but in *fall* in the *Ram*. In the Lion and the Water-bearer (*Aquarius*) no planet stands in exaltation and none in fall, in the Bull none in fall, in the Scorpion none in exaltation. Besides the sun, the moon and the five planets, the nodes of the moon, the dragon's head and the dragon's tail, also have their exaltationes and casus. To the 3rd column corresponds the innermost circle of Batt.'s rose; the 5th column is missing in it. In the rose there is also given the degree of the sign in which the planet reaches the maximum of its power (for example Venus in the 27th degree of the Fishes); these degrees are missing in our table. The headings of these columns too, *regnum* (kingship) for *dignitas* or *exaltatio*, and *servitus* (servitude) for *casus* or *deiectio*, we meet here for the first time.
@p 0136L20..0136L28
Columns 6 and 7 contain the *lords* or *rulers* (here called *Judices*, judges) of the so-called *triplicities* (here called *trigonalia*), that is, of the sets of three signs of the zodiac that form the corners of an equilateral triangle. Thus, for example, the *Ram*, the *Lion* and the *Archer* form a *triplicity* (that is, they stand in trine aspect to one another), and their lords (*Judices*, *domini*) are the *sun*, *Jupiter* and *Saturn* by *day*, and *Jupiter*, the *sun* and *Saturn* by *night*. Strictly, only the first two are the lords; the third, Saturn, is only their (somewhat subordinate) companion. These *Judices* (*domini*) of the triplicities are found in the astrological rose in the second outermost ring, and there too for day and for night.
@p 0136L29..0136L40
The lower half of the table contains on the left the so-called *facies* (faces) (*decani*, Arab. *wajh*, pl. *wujūh*) and their lords (*domini*). For each sign of the zodiac is divided into three equal parts of 10° each, and to each of these parts a planet (the sun and the moon included) is assigned as lord. This distribution was not carried out on the same principle by all the peoples who have occupied themselves with astrology; the distribution given here is the Egyptian-Greek one[^104.1], which has been adopted by most of the Arab astrologers: to the first decan of the Ram its lord, Mars, is assigned, to the second the sun, to the third Venus, to the first decan of the Bull Mercury, and so on, always in the old order of these heavenly bodies: Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, sun, Venus, Mercury, moon. — The astrological rose contains the facies in the 3rd (middle) ring, distributed in the same way as in our table.
@p 0136L41 + 0137L01..0137L12
On the right in the lower half of the table the *bounds* (*termini*, here called *fines*, Arab. *ḥadd*, pl. *ḥudūd*) are entered. Each sign of the zodiac is also divided into five parts, in general unequal, and to each of them one of the five planets (the sun and the moon drop out here) is assigned as lord. Thus to the first 6° of the Ram *Jupiter* is assigned, to the next 6° *Venus*, to the following 8° *Mercury*, to the further 5° *Mars* and to the last 5° *Saturn*. Here too various systems of distribution existed; the one followed here differs from that of Batt.'s rose (in the outermost ring), and seems to be the system that Nallino[^105.1] ascribes to the Egyptians, which was also adopted by most of the Arab astrologers. The title of this part of the table is *Fines secundum Medos* (the bounds according to the Medes); the author, then, ascribes this method of distribution to the Medes; does he mean by this the Babylonians or the Persians? Batt. in his tables of kings (II, 2) calls a Darius, the predecessor of Cyrus, a Mede (*Medus*), and so does al-Bīrūnī in his Chronology.[^105.2]

If at the end we try to survey what the study of this work has yielded us about the sources that Kh. followed, we must admit that the result is not a decided one, and is certain only in a few points. It is certain that in determining the *mean places* of the planets he adopted the *Indo-Persian* procedure, that is, took the sidereal year as his basis; further, that in the size of the *obliquity* of the *ecliptic* he followed Ptol. It is very probable[^105.3] that in computing the *equations* of the *planets* he went the way of the *Persians*, that is, of the *Tables* of the *Shāh*, which in turn are based on the *Siddhāntas* of the *Indians*; on which of them cannot be said with certainty. *Persian* traces we notice, further, in the tables of the *true* or *unequal motion* of the sun and the moon, as the retention of the expression *buht*, which Batt. does not know, proves. In the tables of the *conjunctions* and *oppositions* (*syzygies*) Kh. (or Maslama?) probably followed mainly Ptol.; in the computation of the *eclipses* and of the *parallax of the moon* we again find an approach to the *Indians*. That Kh. may have kept more to the indirect Persian sources than to the direct Indian ones is probable, and also easily explained, since he was indeed a Persian by birth; it is also possible that in the first edition of his tables he leaned still more on the Indian *Siddhāntas*, and in the second more on the *Tables* of the *Shāh*, on Ptol., and perhaps also on the *tested tables* of al-Maʾmūn's astronomers.
@p 0138L04..0138L17
Our knowledge of the changes that Maslama al-Majrīṭī made in Kh.'s tables is likewise deficient in certain points. We can assert with certainty only that Maslama, in the tables for determining the *mean places* of the planets, replaced the *Persian era* of Yazdegerd by the *Muslim* one, and that he reworked for the meridian and the latitude of *Córdoba* the tables of the *syzygies*, the *eclipses* and the *parallax of the moon* (if these were present at all in Kh.'s original work), and likewise the tables of the *distribution* of the twelve *astrological houses* and those of the *proiectiones radiorum*. As for the latter tables, he made, according to what we know from Ibn Hibintā (see above, p. 100), some further changes in them. Added by Maslama himself are perhaps (this too is not certain) some of Tables 1 to 3$^{a}$ (see above, pp. 40–41), Table 60 of the *cotangents*, Tables 67–68 of the *equation of time*, and the two last Tables 115 and 116, on the *conversion* of the *years of birth* and on the *domicilia*, *exaltationes*, etc.

To make the judgement of what Kh.'s work may originally have contained, and what not, more difficult still, there is finally the further fact that the translator (or translators), perhaps also later revisers and arrangers of the translation (Robert of Chester), or copyists too, made additions to the text and also to some of the tables, and that these additions are not always as easy to recognize as such as is the case with some passages of the text that were discussed above, pp. 35 and 37.
@p 0138L25..0138L41
But in spite of this manifold work of collaboration and alteration on these tables, they nevertheless keep, even in the form in which they lie before us, a value beside those of Batt. that is not to be underrated: they very probably form the basis of later Western Arabic tables, and so perhaps also of certain tables of the Christian Middle Ages; direct proofs of this view, to be sure, we cannot yet bring forward, but they may well still be found; perhaps it is precisely the tables of Roger of Hereford mentioned in the introduction that rest on the present ones. They show us further, very clearly, the great progress that Arabic astronomy made from Kh. to Batt.: in the latter, trigonometric formulas and mathematical proofs for the problems; in the former, only bare, short rules without any explanation, and very few traces of trigonometric derivation. If, then, on the one hand the greatness of the work of the most important Arab astronomer stands out all the more brilliantly through the publication of these tables, on the other hand they prove in turn that even at that time one knew how to produce, with quite elementary mathematical means, astronomical tables that, as we have seen, were able to satisfy no small demands of accuracy.
