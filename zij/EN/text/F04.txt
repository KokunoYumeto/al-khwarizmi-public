Our treatise occupies fol. 8$^{r}$–68$^{v}$ of Ms. N. Besides it, this codex contains a series of mostly anonymous texts and tables of astronomical and astrological content, of which only those that immediately follow our treatise (fol. 69–84, that is, to the end) concern us: they are tables on various astronomical subjects, especially on eclipses, composed by the Englishman Roger of Hereford (c. 1150–1220)[^XII.4] for the latitude and longitude of that place, which are given as 52° and 24° respectively. Thus fol. 71 says, for example: *Sunt autem hae tabulae compositae super Herefordiam a magistro Rogerio Herefordensi, sicut et praemissa tabula de eclipsi solis et regulae praescriptae. Est autem longitudo Herefordiae ab occidente arcus XXIV graduum, latitudo vero LII*[^XII.5] (these tables were composed for Hereford by Master Roger of Hereford, as were the preceding table of the eclipse of the sun and the rules written before it; the longitude of Hereford from the west is an arc of 24 degrees, and its latitude 52). — On fol. 73 there follows: *Tabula medii motus solis super mediam noctem Herefordiae secundum annos domini* (table of the mean motion of the sun for midnight at Hereford, by the years of the Lord). — These tables may be regarded as supplements to Adelard's translation, for precisely this part of the work, on the eclipses, leaves something to be desired, since the tables of the *ascensiones obliquae* for *Córdoba* are missing; Roger of Hereford has indeed supplied this, but not for Córdoba: for his home town.

How, then, are these manuscripts related to one another? As I have mentioned above, M is most probably a careless copy of C. As for N, we are of the opinion that Robert of Chester had both C and O, or their archetypes, before him: in the text he follows C almost word for word, with only a few deviations; in the figures of the tables he follows now the reading of C, now that of O, but the former more often. Robert's correcting hand shows itself above all in his having replaced, with very few exceptions, the many Arabic words that C and O leave untranslated or keep beside the translation by Latin ones: thus for *dakaicae* he always has *minuta*, for *eltheniae* *secunda*, for *elwacat* *medius cursus*, for *tadil* *coaequatio* (not *aequatio*), for *elgeib* *sinus*, for *elfelek elmustakim* *circulus aequatorialis* or *aequinoctialis*, etc.
@p 0017L04..0017L35
The relation of C to O, and the relation of both to Adelard's autograph, is the hardest to explain. Bjørnbo, who left no written statement on the matter, was of the opinion, according to a letter to me from Prof. Heiberg in Copenhagen, “that we have only copies of two unfinished working copies of the translator, so that the restitution of an original text would be impossible; rather, in many cases the two texts would have to be printed side by side.” Yet the two texts C and O do not differ from each other so much that this would be necessary; Bjørnbo himself did it in one single chapter only (no. 7), and we have followed him in this. He is right that a restitution of the original text is impossible, and that very probably the text and the tables are incomplete in C and O too, though only slightly. But the deviations are nevertheless such that they cannot come from the mere copying of one manuscript from the other; and so the conjecture has forced itself upon us that the scribe of O or of C — which of the two we cannot decide — had another translation before him besides Adelard's, and took his text now from the one, now from the other, whichever seemed to him the better. It is indeed very probable that Hermannus Dalmata, the contemporary and friend of Robert of Chester, also translated the tables of al-Khwārizmī (as altered by Maslama). In a passage of the translation of Ptolemy's *Planisphaerium* with Maslama's commentary, which was long wrongly ascribed to Rudolf of Bruges, Hermannus says: *Quem locum a Ptolemaeo minus diligenter perspectum cum Albatene miratur et Alchoarismus, quorum hunc quidem opera nostra Latium habet, illius vero commodissima translatio Roberti mei industria, Latinae orationis thesaurum accumulat*[^XIII.1] (al-Khwārizmī, together with al-Battānī, wonders at this place, which Ptolemy examined with too little care; of these two, Latium has the former through our labour, while a most convenient translation of the latter, by the diligence of my Robert, adds to the treasure of the Latin tongue). Might the different rendering of the Arabic letter *shīn* in the two manuscripts C and O, by *sh* (English) and by *sc* (Dalmatian-Italian?) respectively, be connected with this? I must leave it to the philologists to pursue this question further and, if possible, to decide it. There is then the further question whether Hermannus Dalmata also brought Adelard's translation into his comparison; as a friend of Robert of Chester, its “arranger”, he will surely have known this translation.
