Now the values of the latitude of the planets computed according to Batt. by no means agree with those computed according to the *Sūrya-Siddhānta*; but the latter do not agree with those computed according to Kh. either. We have already remarked above that Kh.'s rule shows no direct agreement with that of the *Sūrya-Siddhānta*; we shall now try to show how far they do agree.
@p 0094L09..0094L10
We have found that the numbers of the last column of Tables 27–56, which bears the heading *Latitudo secunda*, are obtained by the formula
@c 0094L11
sin *lat. sec.* = sin *argum.* × sin *lat. maxim.*,

where the *argumentum* = the true longitude of the planet less the longitude of its node = the distance of the planet from the node, and the *lat. max.* (at the mean distance) = the *lat. secunda* for 90° (for Saturn = 5° according to Kh.); this *lat. secunda*, then, agrees with the numerator of the value for the latitude at the true distance (see p. 61)[^62.1]. Now Kh.'s rule says that one must divide the *lat. secunda* by the *lat. prima* to obtain the latitude sought: not by the *lat. prima* that corresponds to the preceding argument (the distance of the planet from the node), but by the one that corresponds to the argument *anomalia aequata* (*elheza examinatum* (!) in the text). If one compares this with the formula given in the *Sūrya-Siddhānta* for the latitude at the true distance, this *lat. prima*, which corresponds to the *anomalia aequata*, would then have to be equal to the variable radius *r′*. The tables of the planets (nos. 27–56), however, do not contain this *r′* in absolute magnitude, but give its ratio to the mean radius *r*; in other words, the numbers of the column *lat. prima* represent the values of *r′* when *r* is taken = 1.
@p 0094L26..0094L30
How this *r′* was determined we could not find out; but in any case it is wrong that the column of the *lat. prima* is headed with degrees and minutes: *r′*, or rather $\dfrac{r'}{r}$, is a pure number, and so instead of *degrees* and *minutes* it should read *partes* and *minutae*; one then also obtains degrees and minutes again for the quotient of the *lat. sec.* by the *lat. prima*.

If one compares our rule with those in Ptol. and Batt., which agree fairly closely, one sees that they behave in exactly opposite ways as regards the arguments for the latitude and the proportional numbers: Ptol. determines the latitude number with the *anomalia aequata* as argument, and the proportional number (by which the latitude found must be multiplied) with the true longitude of the planet increased by 50° (for Saturn)[^63.1] as argument; Kh., on the other hand, determines the latitude number with the distance of the planet from the ascending node (that is, indirectly with the true longitude), but the proportional number (*latit. prima*) with the *anomalia aequata* as argument. These different conceptions then naturally entail a quite different arrangement of the tables as well; we shall mention only one main difference: for the upper planets Ptol. and Batt. have the maximum of the latitude at the argument 180° and the minimum at 0° (360°); Kh., on the other hand, has the maximum of the *latitudo secunda* at 90° (270°) and the minimum at 0° and 180°.
