The words *dicta autem era ex quo . . . reipublicae* are indeed a quotation from Isidore of Seville, which L. Ideler also cites in his attempts to explain the word *aera*[^XVIII.2]. The explanation of *bissextus* is hardly intelligible, and was probably misunderstood by the scribe too. As is well known, the word comes from the fact that in leap years one said *twice*: *Sextus ante Kalendas Martii*, that is, one counted 24 February *twice*. The scribe takes the *Nonae Martii* instead of the *Kalendae Martii*, and with what follows he probably means that the leap day was intercalated at various places, even at the end of December, which, as we shall see below, was the case in the so-called Spanish era. — What is meant by the words *in cursu lunae* we do not understand.

7) At the lower margin of fol. 18$^{v}$ (Table 15) there is the following note: *Adiectiones Veneris et Mercurii sunt adiectio solis; hae autem adiectiones omnes iuxta civitatem Londoniae in hoc libro computantur et mediis cursibus planetarum adiciuntur* (sic) (the additions of Venus and Mercury are the addition of the sun; all these additions are computed in this book for the city of London and are added to the mean courses of the planets). — By this the author of the note, and this is without doubt Robert of Chester[^XVIII.3], means to say that the mean longitudes of Venus and Mercury are the same as that of the sun, and that for these and for the three upper planets he has computed the *adiectiones* for the meridian of London. By these *adiectiones* the following is to be understood. The mean longitudes of the sun, the moon and the planets computed from Tables 9–20 hold for the meridian of *Arin* (*Ujjain*); for London a certain quantity must be added to the mean longitude of each of these heavenly bodies, namely its mean motion (*motus medius*) in c. 5$^{h}$ (for Ujjain is c. 75° east of London); this quantity to be added Robert of Chester calls *adiectio*. — He has given these *adiectiones* in several places (each time at the lower margin of the leaf): for Saturn on fol. 24$^{v}$ (Table 9) as 18″, for Jupiter on fol. 28$^{v}$ (Table 11) as 45″, for Mars on fol. 14$^{v}$ (Table 13) as 4′ 49″, and for the dragon's head on fol. 23$^{v}$ (Table 19) as 30″; the tables for the mean motion of the sun and of the moon are missing in N. These figures, however, do not agree with our tables for a difference in time of 5$^{h}$; Robert of Chester seems to have taken it as not quite 4$^{h}$. The approximately correct figures, from Tables 10, 12, 14 and 20, are: Saturn 25″, Jupiter 1′ 2″, Mars 6′ 33″, the dragon's head 40″ (for the sun 12′ 19″, for the moon 2° 44′ 42″).

10) On fol. 42$^{r}$, after the words *Sed et aequalibus idem e contrario addendo inaequales fient* (but by adding the same to the equal ones, conversely, they will become unequal; in our text p. 25), a second hand has added: *Verus dies naturalis medius est XXIIII horarum, id est revolutio CCCLX graduum et LIX min. et V sec. Unde* (or *una*) *hora est XV grad. et II. min. et XXVIII sec. Sed quoniam gradus aequinoctialis lineae, secundum quam haec determinata* (?) *sunt, et zodiaci in quo sol movetur, non similiter transeunt meridianam lineam, sed et sol aliquando citius aliquando tardius contra celum movetur, necesse est ergo naturali diei medio aliquando addi, aliquando subtrahi. Attamen haec tabula sic facta est, quod semper subtrahitur a die media, sed aliquando sequenti die maius aliquando minus quam priori. Sed quando maius tunc ille dies aequatus a meridiana linea in meridianam erit minor die media, si autem minus tunc necesse est esse maiorem; maior enim subtractio minuit de medio, minor vero subtractio de medio et aequali residuum relinquit maius quam faceret subtractio maior. Exempli gratia: si hodie a revolutione sperae*[^XIX.1] *et LIX min. et VIII sec. subtraherentur II min. et VIII sec., et in sequenti die III min. et VIII sec., esset tunc dies aequatus revolutio sperae et LVIII min., quod est minus die medio; item si priori die III min. et VIII sec. subtraherentur et sequenti die tantum II min. et VIII sec., erit dies aequatus revolutio sperae et LX min., quod est maius die medio* (the true mean natural day is of 24 hours, that is, a revolution of 360 degrees and 59 min. and 5 sec. Hence (or: one) hour is 15 deg. and 2 min. and 28 sec. But since the degrees of the equinoctial line, by which these things are determined (?), and of the zodiac, in which the sun moves, do not cross the meridian line alike, and the sun moreover moves against the heaven now faster, now slower, something must therefore be added to the mean natural day at one time, subtracted at another. This table, however, is so made that something is always subtracted from the mean day, but on the following day sometimes more, sometimes less than on the one before. And when more, that equated day from the meridian line to the meridian line will be less than the mean day; when less, it must be greater; for the greater subtraction diminishes the mean day, while the smaller subtraction from the mean and equal day leaves a greater remainder than the greater subtraction would. For example: if today 2 min. and 8 sec. were subtracted from a revolution of the sphere and 59 min. and 8 sec., and on the following day 3 min. and 8 sec., the equated day would then be a revolution of the sphere and 58 min., which is less than the mean day; likewise, if 3 min. and 8 sec. were subtracted on the earlier day and only 2 min. and 8 sec. on the following one, the equated day will be a revolution of the sphere and 60 min., which is greater than the mean day).
