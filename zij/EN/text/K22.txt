As we have remarked above, the two chapters 34 and 35 are very unclear, and most probably incomplete and in part wrongly rendered in the translation. We shall hardly go wrong if we assume that Adelard did not properly understand these somewhat difficult astronomical sections, and later copyists, naturally, still much less; from this resulted partly false interpretations, partly omissions, two of which I wish to mention here and to try to correct. In chapter 34 (p. 28) it says: *erit hoc productum horis coniunctionis addendum* (this product is to be added to the hours of the conjunction); instead of *horis coniunctionis* it must probably read *loco coniunctionis* (to the place of the conjunction), or better *loco vero lunae* (to the true place of the moon); for here it is not specifically a matter of a conjunction, but generally of the place of the moon as changed by the *parallax*. And further it says in the same place: *Quodque inde surget, horam eclipsis supra locum pro quo inquiris ex diversitate aspectus mutatam ponit* (and what results from this gives the hour of the eclipse, as changed by the parallax, for the place for which you are inquiring); instead of *horam eclipsis* it must read: *locum lunae apparentem in ecliptica* (the apparent place of the moon in the ecliptic)[^96.1]. In chapter 35 the passage from the beginning to *Si enim fuerit locus coniunctionis* (for if the place of the conjunction is) is quite insufficient for determining an eclipse of the sun; but I shall not be blamed if I refrain from an attempt to restore the right text.

@c 0128L24
(Text pp. 25 and 29–30, Tables 79–90.)
@p 0128L25..0128L29
These two chapters and the last, which follows next, deal with purely *astrological* questions. Chapter 32 stands only in Mss. C and N, and in the former in the wrong place[^96.2]; it should immediately precede chapter 36, for it contains the precepts for finding the beginnings of the twelve houses on the ecliptic, chapter 36, on the other hand, the instructions for using the tables that belong to them.
@p 0128L30..0128L37 + 0129L01..0129L19
The Arabic *taswiya* = distribution, division, arrangement has been rendered by the translator partly by *aequatio*, partly by the more correct *dispositio*. It is a matter of the distribution of the twelve astrological houses over the celestial sphere. Various kinds of this distribution existed, for which I refer to Batt.[^96.3]; all have in common that six great circles of the celestial sphere intersect in one diameter, by which the sphere is divided into twelve spherical lunes. Batt.'s division, which is probably also the Ptolemaic one in the *Quadripartitum*, and which Kh. too follows, is the following. The six great circles pass through the poles of the equator, and are thus meridians or circles of declination; the first, passing through the *ascendant*, marks the beginning of house I. Now, to obtain the point on the ecliptic through which the second circle passes (counted from the ascendant towards the east, in the order of the signs of the zodiac), with which, then, house II begins, one must divide the night arc of the ascendant by 6[^97.1], add the result to the right ascension of the ascendant, and look up the longitude for this new right ascension in Tables 59–59$^{b}$; one then has the degree of the beginning of house II, and, increased by 180°, the degree of the beginning of house VIII, which is the nadir (*naẓīr*) of that of house II. If one wants the beginning of house III (or IX), one adds the 6th part of the night arc once more to the right ascension found just now, and the sum is the right ascension of the beginning of house III (or IX); the same holds also for computing the beginning of house IV (or X). To obtain the beginning of house V (or XI), one must add to the right ascension of house IV the 6th part of the day arc of the ascendant (= $\tfrac{360^{\circ} - \text{night arc}}{6}$ = 60° − $\tfrac{\text{night arc}}{6}$), and again look up the longitude for this right ascension; this is the beginning of house V (or XI). One now proceeds in the same way for finding the beginnings of houses VI and VII; if the computation has been carried out correctly, the beginning of house VII must be the nadir of the beginning of house I, that is, of the ascendant.
@p 0129L20..0129L25
One sees from this, then, that these twelve houses were of unequal size; still, houses I, II, III, VII, VIII and IX are congruent with one another, and so are houses IV, V, VI, X, XI and XII. What is peculiar is that smaller and larger houses fall both on the day arc and on the night arc. Each geographical latitude has its own size of the houses, and this changes again with the season; only at the equator are all the houses of equal size at every season[^97.2].
@p 0129L26..0129L36
In chapter 32 it is then also stated how one can determine the size (in degrees) of a *temporal hour*, that is, both of a *day hour* and of a *night hour*; for this one needs the tables of the oblique ascensions for the latitude concerned, but these tables, as has been remarked before, are missing in this work. To make a trial of the beginnings of the twelve houses, we have determined the size of the temporal hour for Córdoba approximately by Batt.'s tables (II, 67), and found it = 12° 4′ for the morning of 23 Jan. 901, that is, for the ascendant 297° 3′ (= 27° 3′ of Capricorn)[^97.3]; one can, to be sure, also compute it trigonometrically, by looking for the hour angle of the sun at its rising on the day concerned at Córdoba. The length of the double day hour is therefore 24° 8′, and the length of the double night hour = 35° 52′.
