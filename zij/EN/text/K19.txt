These are the differences of the three data at the near and the far distance. What, then, do these data mean? Our text tells us nothing about it; but Batt. instructs us in chapter 43.[^87.1] He, like Kh., understands by the *digits* of the eclipse, in a *partial* as well as in a *total* eclipse of the moon, the distance from the edge of the earth's shadow to the edge of the disc of the moon that is convex towards it, in parts of which 12 go to the diameter of the moon.

It is computed in the following way (by Batt.'s simple precept): one adds the two radii, of the shadow and of the moon (in both figures let this be *SC*), subtracts from this sum the latitude of the moon (*SM*), and so has the magnitude of the eclipse in minutes (in Fig. 1 *AM*, in Fig. 2 *CM* = *AB*). @p 0119L22..0119L29 + 0120L01..0120L02
As for the terms *casus* and *mora*, the former is the translation of the Arab. *suqūṭ*, which we must translate here by “falling in”; by this word the Arab astronomers render the Ptolemaic *ἔμπτωσις* = the “falling into”; *casus* therefore means the phase from the beginning of the eclipse to the beginning of totality. The second (*mora*) is the translation of the Arab. *mukth* (or *makth*) = “staying”, which in turn translates the Ptolemaic *μονή*; *mora* therefore means the phase of the total eclipse. — In the text of this chapter there also occurs *respiratio*, as the translation of the Arab. *injilāʾ* = “appearing”, which in turn corresponds to the Ptolemaic *ἀναπλήρωσις* = the “making full again” (of the moon); *emersio* would be better than *respiratio*.
@p 0120L03..0120L15
For computing the minutes of the *casus* and of the *mora* we likewise give Batt.'s rules (I, 98, ll. 5–14), and show their correctness with the help of the following two figures. Batt. says first: from the square of the sum of the two radii (*MS*$^{2}$ in Fig. 3) subtract the square of the latitude of the moon (*SN*$^{2}$); then the root of the difference (thus *MN*), divided by the hourly motion of the moon, gives the time from the beginning of the eclipse to the middle of the total eclipse (thus the *casus* and the *half mora* together). — Secondly he says: if a total eclipse takes place (and one wants to know its time alone), subtract the diameter of the moon (*MT* in Fig. 4) from the sum of the two radii (*ST*)[^88.1], and from the square of this difference (*SM*$^{2}$) subtract the square of the latitude of the moon (*SN*$^{2}$); then the root of the difference (thus *MN*), divided by the hourly motion of the moon, gives half the time of the total eclipse (thus the *half mora*). If one subtracts this from the time obtained by the first rule, one obtains the time of the *casus*.
