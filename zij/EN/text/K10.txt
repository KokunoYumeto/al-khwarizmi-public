@p 0092L03..0092L08
The Latin title of this chapter is incorrect: in its first paragraph the chapter deals with the three upper planets, in the second with the two lower ones. The theory of the change of the latitude of the planets is the most difficult and obscure part (book 13) of Ptol., so difficult and obscure that Batt. renounced going into this theory more closely; he gives only the rules by which the latitude of the planets can be found from the tables[^60.1].
@p 0092L09..0092L27
But this obscurity has been cleared up in an excellent way by Nallino, after Delambre and others had failed at it, and he has thereby brought the theory of Ptol. nearer to our understanding; he has also tried to give it a mathematical foundation, in which he has likewise succeeded finely. We cannot go into these foundations more closely here, but must refer the reader to Nallino's work[^60.2]; we shall only mention briefly the main factors on which the latitude of the planets in their various positions depends. The latitude of the *three upper* planets depends, first, on the constant inclination of the plane of the deferent to the ecliptic, and secondly on the variable inclination of the epicycle to the plane of the deferent. The latitude of the *two lower* planets depends, first, on the inclination of the plane of the deferent to the ecliptic, which Ptol. takes to be variable for the lower planets; secondly, on the variable inclination of the diameter apogee–perigee of the epicycle to the plane of the deferent; and thirdly, on the variable inclination to the plane of the ecliptic of the diameter perpendicular to the one just named (for the three upper planets this diameter is always parallel to the plane of the ecliptic). — The only thing we have to object to in Nallino's accounts is that he has not tried to make Ptol.'s unclear figures more vivid by adding perspective figures.
@p 0092L28..0092L33
If we now compare the rules that Kh. has given for determining the latitude of the planets with those of Batt., we see at once that the former must rest on other theories than the latter; and it is quite understandable that the difficulty of the Ptolemaic account deterred his successors from following the path of the great master and compelled them to use other, simpler methods.

If we now look at the rule of the *Sūrya-Siddhānta*[^60.3], we find no direct agreement with Kh.'s here either; but that both proceeded from similar points of view is nevertheless evident. The rule of the *Sūrya-Siddhānta* says: the sine of the arc (the difference of the true longitude of the planet and the longitude of the node), multiplied by the maximum latitude and divided by the radius (for this position of the planet), gives the latitude for the position (time) concerned. Burgess says in his commentary[^61.1] on this rule that it results from the composition of two proportions, of which the one holds only for the moon, while the other is added for the planets, namely:
