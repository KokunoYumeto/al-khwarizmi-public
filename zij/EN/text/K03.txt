Chapter 5. On finding the Roman year
@c 0070L07
(Text pp. 6–7, Tables 3 and 3$^{a}$.)
@p 0070L08..0070L19
The title is really *De anni bissextilis inventione* (on finding the bissextile year). But in fact it is a matter of converting the years of the *Hijra* into years of the *Seleucid era*, or of finding the *Roman* date that corresponds to a given *Arab* date. The years of the Roman era were also called *anni bissextiles* (leap years), which seems somewhat strange, since the Arabs too had leap years; but this *anni bissextiles* stands in contrast to the *anni vagi* of the Egyptians, who knew no *intercalation*. The corresponding Table 3 is headed *Pagina reducendi annos Alexandri ad elhigeram* (page for reducing the years of Alexander to the Hijra), which is really incorrect. The table is arranged somewhat differently from the corresponding tables of Batt.[^38.3], which give, for the beginning of each year of the *Hijra* from 1 to 600, the corresponding date of the *Roman era*; Kh.'s table takes up much less room, but still requires subsidiary computations. We give three examples of this reduction.

Since a conversion of 12 months into one year has taken place once, one must subtract 5$\tfrac{1}{4}$ days from this sum (for if one takes a year for 12 months, one has taken 5$\tfrac{1}{4}$ days too many)[^38.4]; this gives 1436 y. 3 m. 27 d. Since among the 3 months from October onwards there are two months of 31 days, one must subtract 2 more from the number of days; this gives 25 days. That is, since the beginning of the Roman era (1 Oct. 312 BC) 1436 y. 3 m. 25 d. have elapsed; that is, *1 Muḥarram* 520 AH corresponds to *26 Kānūn II* of the year 1437, that is, to *26 January* 1126 AD.

Table 3$^{a}$ serves to determine whether the Roman year found is a leap year or not, and with which weekday it and its months begin. The columns headed with the names of the months contain the *Signa* (*Notae*). It should be noted that, if one wants the beginning of a month of a given year in the *Julian* reckoning, one has to add 311 (312) to this year number.[^39.2] Further, one must take into account that in the Julian calendar the days of a month fall again on the same weekdays as before after 28 years have passed, so that in Table 3$^{a}$, under *Anni romanorum plani*, only the remainders are entered that result from dividing the year number by 28; instead of the remainder 0 the number 28 stands at the end of the column. To spare this division each time, Table 3 has a column (the last) with the multiples of 28; the same multiples, in considerably greater number, are also found in Table 1. I give three examples.
@p 0072L08..0072L09
1. What weekday was 5 March (Syr. *ādhār*, in our tables written adar) of the year 1363 of the *Roman* era?
@p 0072L10..0072L15
In Table 1 one finds the nearest number below 1363 that is divisible by 28 to be 1344; this taken from 1363 leaves 19; in Table 3$^{a}$ one finds against 19, in the column headed “*Mart.*”, the *nota* 1, that is, 1 March fell on a Sunday, the 5th, then, on a *Thursday*. This year of the Roman era was a leap year, for in the column headed *Anni bissextiles* there stands *bissext.*; and so it was, for it corresponds to the year 1052 of the Julian reckoning.
@p 0072L16..0072L22
2. What weekday was 26 January 1126 of the *Julian* reckoning? We add the number 311 to 1126; this gives 1437; in Table 3 one finds the nearest number divisible by 28 to be 1428; this taken from 1437 leaves 9; in Table 3$^{a}$ one finds against 9, in the column headed January, the *nota* 6, that is, 1 January fell on a Friday, and so did the 8th, the 15th and the 22nd; the 26th, then, on a *Tuesday*. This result agrees with the statement on p. 5 of the text (see also above, p. 37).
@p 0072L23..0072L24
3. What weekday was 10 October of the year 1912 of the *Julian* reckoning?
@p 0072L25..0072L28
We add the number 312 to 1912 (see p. 39); this gives 2224; the nearest number divisible by 28 is 2212 (this number no longer stands in our tables), remainder 12; to it belongs, in Table 3$^{a}$ under October, the nota 2; so 1 October fell on a Monday, and so did the 8th; the 10th, then, on a *Wednesday*.
